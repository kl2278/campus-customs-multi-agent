Reflection

1. How I'd grade the agents, ticket by ticket

I graded the team on four questions: did the cash stay correct, did a human approve every payment, did the agents follow their own rules, and did each ticket end with a complete answer for the customer. On the first two the team was perfect. The Cash tab shows it: $3,400 down to $160 through exactly two payments, and nothing moved until a human approver said so. Completeness is where it lost points.

Ticket 101: B. It got the money right and the order right. The Boss called Inventory first, Inventory found zero tees in size S and picked vendor 1 by its apparel-reprint specialty, Accounting queued the $840 for invoice 501, and nothing moved until I approved it. After my approval the next run created a purchase order for one tee ($8, arriving 2026-09-05, no cash moved). It isn't an A for three reasons. The first attempt crashed on a gateway error before the model answered anything (the audit trail shows no tokens for it). The Boss also re-delegated to Accounting just to confirm things, which cost a whole extra handoff. And customer draft 2 mentioned a pending order and an estimated date, which broke the Customer Service rule, so the two drafts now disagree with each other.

Ticket 102: A. One run, Boss to Facilities to Accounting. The rent was queued at exactly the lease's $2,400, I approved it, cash went down by $2,400, and the lease's next due date moved from 2026-09-02 to 2026-10-02. It took 11 model requests and no limit came close to being hit.

Ticket 103: D+. The price decision itself was fine: $52 per hoodie, which is $30 above the $22 cost and $600 of margin on 20 hoodies (57.69%). But it took three runs. In runs 1 and 2 the Boss deferred the discount to a human, because its prompt told it discounts were a human judgment, and Inventory created two $264 purchase orders (PO 2 and PO 3). I rejected both because cash was $160. Inventory recreated the order after my first rejection because nothing in its prompt said a rejection is final. In run 3 the Boss resolved the ticket, but it never looked at the 12-hoodie shortfall and never called Customer Service, so no customer reply exists. That is why the grade is so low even though the number is right.

3. What would have been simpler as one agent

Ticket 102 is the clearest case. It needed three tools in the end: get_lease_rent_status, get_cash_balance and queue_payment_for_approval. One agent could have done that in a few calls, instead of three agents each reading the ticket and passing summaries along. Ticket 103's price check was a single get_unit_pricing call that went from the Boss to Accounting and back. On ticket 101, the two working runs took 20 and 19 model requests and about 44,000 input tokens each (43,967 and 43,565), and the Boss's extra handoff to Accounting only to confirm invoice 501 was paid was part of that. A single agent with the same tools wouldn't have needed it.

What the team does better is least privilege. Only Accounting can queue and execute payments, and Customer Service has no payment or purchase tools at all. A single agent would need every tool at once. But I noticed that the real safety doesn't come from the agents. It comes from code: the human approve route, the cash check when a payment executes, and the rule that a vendor with an open invoice can't be ordered from. So for simple tickets I'd use one agent plus those code guards, and keep separate agents only where the roles need different permissions or different prompts.

4. Three new problems this team could solve

Another rent notice or lease coming due. Facilities would read the lease with get_lease_rent_status, Accounting would check cash and queue the payment with get_cash_balance and queue_payment_for_approval, and a human approves it. Ticket 102 is the proof.

Another customer order that is short on stock for an apparel item. The team can check stock, list the vendors, check whether the vendor can ship, create a purchase order that waits for approval, and draft a customer reply. Ticket 101 is the proof, as long as the vendor has no open invoice.

Another vendor invoice that goes overdue and blocks shipments. The team can list the open invoices, see which vendor can't ship, and queue the payment for approval after checking it against cash. Invoice 501 on ticket 101 is the proof.

5. Three new problems it couldn't solve, and what I'd build

Receiving stock. The team can create a purchase order and get it approved, but nothing marks it as arrived or raises inventory. Tee S is still at 0 even though PO 1 was approved. I'd add a receive_purchase_order tool that updates the inventory, and either a Receiving role or that tool given to Inventory with a human check.

Planning cash across everything that is pending. Approving a purchase order doesn't check cash, which is why I had to reject two $264 orders by hand while cash was $160. I'd add a forecast_cash tool that subtracts pending purchase orders and upcoming rent from the balance, and a Treasury or Planner agent that tells the Boss what is affordable before anything is queued.

Talking to vendors and customers for real, and bringing money in. Drafts stay on the board and no revenue is modeled, so the team can't negotiate a payment plan with vendor 1 while invoice 501 was open, send the customer's reply, or record a sale at the $52 hoodie price. I'd add a send_message tool with mandatory human review, a record_sale and receivables tool, and a Sales or Outreach agent, all behind the same approval gate I use for payments.
